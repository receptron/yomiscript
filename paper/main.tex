\title{The LLM as the Front End:\\
Context-Aware Pronunciation for Small Browser Text-to-Speech, with Japanese Homographs}
\author{\todo{authors and affiliations}}
\date{Draft, 1 November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Text-to-speech (TTS) must decide how each word is read, and in Japanese the reading often depends on the context:
homographs such as 上手 and 人気, numbers with counters, dates, and acronyms. Dictionary front ends choose from the
sentence alone. We let an LLM, given the text and the context that comes with it, decide the reading and write it in
YomiScript, a notation built on IPA and BCP~47 that separates deciding the reading (the \emph{Yomite}) from turning it
into sound (the \emph{Katarite}). A Katarite for Kokoro-82M then needs no text analysis and runs in a browser. On a
70-sentence Japanese development set, YomiScript from three LLMs was read correctly in 98.1\% of sentences, against
78.6\% for pyopenjtalk and 32.9\% for Kokoro's front end; on an independent benchmark of 3{,}000 annotated sentences
with frozen prompts, Claude's and Codex's kana error rate was 0.63--0.87\% as YomiScript and 0.25--0.27\% as kana,
against 1.05\% for pyopenjtalk. On 130 sentences ambiguous alone, the LLMs read 48.5\% right
without context and 97.7\% given the preceding paragraph; an LLM that wrote a chat answer and a reader
given the conversation read it equally often right (no difference detected). Kana is the more accurate and a fifth
the size for Japanese readings, but
cannot carry accent or English: with accent marks, Kokoro's pitch separated Tokyo minimal pairs at 92--95\% of points,
against 42\% with its own pitch. The results are preliminary: small sets, gold reviewed by one annotator.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Speech interfaces to LLMs usually send the generated text to a cloud speech API. Moving synthesis onto the device,
into the browser tab that shows the chat, removes the network round trip and the per-character cost, and small
open models such as Kokoro-82M~\citep{kokoro82m} now make this practical. What does not move easily is the front end:
the grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) step that decides how each word is read. For English it is a pronunciation dictionary
plus rules; for Japanese it is a morphological analyser with a large dictionary. Kokoro's own Japanese front end
(misaki~\citep{misaki}, built on MeCab~\citep{kudo2004mecab} with UniDic 3.1.0~\citep{den2008unidic}) needs a
connection-cost table of 481\,MB (413\,MB compressed with gzip) and a 243\,MB dictionary, too large to ship to a
browser. Smaller front ends do fit (piper-plus~\citep{piperplus} compiles OpenJTalk to WebAssembly in about 19\,MB), so
size is not the main obstacle.

Even where a front end fits, it reads the sentence, not what it means in its context. In Japanese the problem is common: 一日 is
\emph{tsuitachi} (the first day of the month) or \emph{ichinichi} (one day), 上手 is \emph{jōzu} (skilful),
\emph{kamite} (stage left) or \emph{uwate} (superior), 人気 is \emph{ninki} (popularity) or \emph{hitoke} (signs of
people). OpenJTalk~\citep{openjtalk} and its Python wrapper pyopenjtalk~\citep{pyopenjtalk} choose the reading
from the dictionary and the local morphological context (the segmentation and the neighbouring words), not from what
the discourse means; on our hand-made hard set pyopenjtalk reads 60.0\% of the homograph sentences correctly
under the scoring of Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

The observation behind this work is simple: an LLM reads text as a person does, from its meaning and from whatever
context comes with it, the surrounding conversation or document. If an LLM decides the reading and hands the TTS a
resolved pronunciation, the hardest part of the front end is done by a component that has the context, whether the
text came from elsewhere or the LLM wrote it itself; in an LLM application the writer already has the conversation and
can write the reading in the same call. What remains for the TTS is to turn a resolved pronunciation into sound, which
a small acoustic model can do in the browser.

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{YomiScript}, a notation for TTS built on existing international standards, IPA~\citep{ipa1999handbook}
  and BCP~47~\citep{rfc5646}, with accent, stress and tone
  marks, phrase boundaries, optional pitch values and an optional original-text layer, which separates the
  \emph{Yomite} (text to YomiScript: an LLM, rules, a dedicated model or a person) from the \emph{Katarite}
  (YomiScript to sound) (Section~\ref{sec:yomiscript}).
  \item A \textbf{Kokoro Katarite} that, given YomiScript written from Kokoro's own front end, hands the model exactly
  the phoneme string and ids of the original pipeline, with identical durations and F0, and audio whose correlation
  with the original is similar to that of a second run of the original (Section~\ref{sec:parity}).
  \item An evaluation of \textbf{LLMs as the Yomite} for Kokoro on Japanese and English, against the original front
  end and pyopenjtalk, on readings, faithfulness of the text layer, intelligibility and cost
  (Section~\ref{sec:results}); the hard-set results are development results, since the prompt was revised on the same
  sets.
  \item A comparison of the \textbf{container}: the same LLMs writing YomiScript, kana, fully annotated SSML and
  sparse kana annotations (Section~\ref{sec:representation}).
  \item Experiments on \textbf{how much context the Yomite needs}: three LLMs (Claude, Codex, Gemini) reading external
  text with and without the preceding paragraph and chat answers with and without the conversation, and as the
  author writing the reading with its own answer (65 homographs, the main comparison stated in advance;
  Section~\ref{sec:author}).
  \item A comparison with the newest \textbf{commercial TTS} (OpenAI, Gemini, ElevenLabs) on the same sentences, judged
  automatically from the audio by a kana speech recogniser on minimal pairs, and checked against Kokoro recordings
  whose readings are known (Section~\ref{sec:commercial}).
  \item A \textbf{second Katarite} for an engine that reads characters rather than IPA (Supertonic~3~\citep{supertonic3}),
  which uses the text layer and gives kana only for words whose written form is ambiguous in a list it carries.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Japanese front ends.} OpenJTalk~\citep{openjtalk} (and pyopenjtalk~\citep{pyopenjtalk}) analyses text with
MeCab and a NAIST-JDIC-based dictionary and produces readings, accent phrases and accent nuclei; it is the de facto
front end of open Japanese TTS. misaki~\citep{misaki}, the G2P of Kokoro, reads Japanese with UniDic through a
romanisation-based path. MARINE~\citep{park2022marine} estimates accent with multi-task learning. zenz~\citep{zenz}
models the reverse direction (kana-kanji conversion) with a small GPT-2 model. These use the local context of the
sentence (segmentation, neighbouring words, parts of speech, or a learned model of them), but none is given the
discourse or the writer's intent.

\paragraph{Neural G2P, homographs and text normalisation.} Multilingual neural G2P such as ByT5-based
models~\citep{zhu2022byt5g2p} learns pronunciations from lexicons. Homograph disambiguation with contextual
embeddings has been studied for English TTS~\citep{nicolis2021homograph}, and text normalisation (numbers, dates,
symbols into words) is a long-standing hard problem~\citep{sproat2016normalization}. For Japanese, features from
pre-trained language models (BERT, Flair) added to morphological analysis improved polyphone disambiguation and accent
prediction by 5.7 and 6.0 points~\citep{hida2022polyphone}. LLMs have since been used in the front end directly. For
English homographs, an LLM that picks the meaning while the pronunciation comes from a dictionary reduced the phoneme
error rate by 28.9\% relative~\citep{han2024ickr}. PolyNorm normalises text for TTS with few-shot prompting and reduces
the word error rate against a production system in eight languages~\citep{wong2025polynorm}. Closest to us,
\citet{koriyama2026g2p} benchmarks over 30 LLMs on Japanese G2P (3{,}000 hand-annotated sentences): the best reach a
kana character error rate below 0.52\%, against 1.03\% for the best conventional tool, and kana predicted by an LLM and
given to a kana-input TTS is pronounced more accurately than an end-to-end TTS reading the text. We differ from these
works in three ways. (1) \emph{What the reader is given}: they read a sentence; we give the LLM the context the text
comes with and measure how much it matters: on sentences ambiguous alone, from 48.5\% to 97.7\% with the preceding
paragraph (Section~\ref{sec:author}), and the LLM that wrote a text has that context without being given it.
(2) \emph{What is handed over}: they produce one TTS's input (kana for a kana-input TTS, normalised text, dictionary
phonemes for an English front end); YomiScript is a notation built on IPA and BCP~47 that is not tied to one engine's
input. We show it driving two engines (Kokoro and Supertonic~3) in two languages (Japanese and English). For Japanese
alone, kana is a smaller container for the same decision (Section~\ref{sec:representation}).
(3) \emph{What the TTS keeps}: their TTS still has a front end. By \emph{front end} we mean the text analysis that
decides how written text is read (G2P and text normalisation). The Kokoro Katarite has none: it maps resolved IPA to
Kokoro's phonemes. The Supertonic Katarite decides no reading either, but it is not free of text processing: it looks
up written forms in an ambiguity list to choose which spans to give as kana from the YomiScript reading, and leaves
the other spans to the engine, which reads characters itself (Section~\ref{sec:yomiscript}). Dropping the dictionary
front end is what lets the Kokoro Katarite run in a browser.

\paragraph{Pronunciation markup.} SSML~\citep{ssml11}, the W3C markup for speech synthesis, wraps text in elements
that adjust how it is spoken. Its \texttt{phoneme} element (\texttt{<phoneme alphabet="ipa" ph="...">}) gives the
pronunciation of a word in IPA, and an SSML document may annotate every word this way, so SSML~1.1 can carry the same
per-word IPA. The difference is in the serialisation, not in what can be expressed. In SSML the text is the content and
the pronunciation an optional attribute, so an engine must be able to read any word left unannotated; in YomiScript
the pronunciation of the whole utterance is the content, every word resolved, and the original text is an optional
annotation of it, so a Katarite never has to read text. YomiScript is also more compact than per-word markup, which
repeats the element and its attributes for every word; Section~\ref{sec:representation} compares the two as written by
LLMs. YomiScript keeps the
symbols IPA already has with their IPA meaning (stress, length, the major and minor group boundaries \ys{‖}
and \ys{|}, downstep \ys{ꜜ} and upstep \ys{ꜛ}, the global fall and rise \ys{↘}/\ys{↗}, tone letters), names the
language with a BCP~47 tag~\citep{rfc5646}, and adds only what TTS needs and IPA lacks: utterance framing, pitch
values and the text layer. A Katarite for a phoneme-input engine therefore needs no text analysis, and the same
YomiScript can drive engines with different phoneme sets.

\paragraph{Small and browser TTS.} Kokoro-82M~\citep{kokoro82m} is a StyleTTS~2-style~\citep{li2023styletts2} model with
an iSTFTNet~\citep{kaneko2022istftnet} decoder that takes phonemes. Piper~\citep{piper}, based on VITS~\citep{kim2021vits},
runs in the browser through ONNX Runtime~\citep{onnxruntime}; piper-plus~\citep{piperplus} adds Japanese with OpenJTalk
compiled to WebAssembly. kokoro-js~\citep{kokorojs} runs Kokoro in the browser with English voices only.
SupertonicTTS~\citep{kim2025supertonic} reads raw characters (no G2P), learns text-speech alignment with flow
matching~\citep{lipman2023flow} and predicts only the utterance duration; Supertonic~3~\citep{supertonic3} covers 31
languages including Japanese. Phonon~\citep{phonon} is a 31M/40M on-device model with a WebAssembly build, without
Japanese at launch. The Web Speech API~\citep{webspeech} exposes the operating system's voices. In all of them the
reading is decided inside the TTS, by a dictionary front end or by the acoustic model itself.

\section{YomiScript}\label{sec:yomiscript}

\paragraph{Roles.} A \emph{Yomite} (読み手, the reader) turns text into YomiScript; a \emph{Katarite} (語り手, the
narrator) turns YomiScript into sound. Lexicons and reading hints are inputs to the Yomite and are not part of
YomiScript, which holds only resolved pronunciation.

\paragraph{Notation.} YomiScript is built on IPA~\citep{ipa1999handbook} and BCP~47~\citep{rfc5646}: symbols that IPA
already has keep their IPA meaning, and only conventions TTS needs and IPA lacks are added. An utterance is a BCP~47
language tag and the pronunciation between slashes, ending in a fall
(\ys{↘}) or rise (\ys{↗}). Every language is written in IPA, Japanese included, mora by mora, with \ys{ꜛ} after the
mora where the pitch rises and \ys{ꜜ} after the accent nucleus; English uses \ys{ˈ}/\ys{ˌ}. \ys{|} is an accent-phrase
boundary and \ys{‖} a breath group with a pause. Optional pitch values \ys{<f0:v>} give a target in semitones relative
to the speaker's register for the unit before them; a Katarite that has its own prosody model may ignore marks and fill
pitch itself. Examples from the specification:

\begin{quote}
\ys{ja /kʲoꜜːwa | joꜜi | teꜛŋkidesɯ ↘/}\\
\ys{en-US /ˈaɪ ˈɹɛd ðə ˈbʊk | ˈjɛstɚˌdeɪ ↘/}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Original text.} \ys{<t:…>} closes a span: the units since the previous \ys{<t:…>} are the reading of that
text, and a \ys{<t:…>} with no units before it is text that is not spoken (punctuation). Joining the values gives back
the text. The layer never changes the pronunciation: the parser removes it before reading the IPA, and Kokoro's input
is identical with or without it (checked on the 48 test fixtures recorded from the original Kokoro pipeline). It serves two purposes:
showing the text while it is spoken (a chat client highlighting the current word), and Katarites whose engine reads
characters. An LLM output from our hard set, for 一日から三日まで休みです (``closed from the 1st to the 3rd''):

\begin{quote}
\ys{ja /tsɯitatɕi<t:一日> kaɾa<t:から> mikka<t:三日> made<t:まで> jasɯmi<t:休み> desɯ<t:です。> ↘/}
\end{quote}

and for 舞台の上手から役者が登場した (``an actor entered from stage left''), where 上手 is \emph{kamite}:

\begin{quote}
\ys{ja /bɯtai<t:舞台> no<t:の> kamite<t:上手> kaɾa<t:から> jakɯɕa<t:役者> ɡa<t:が> toːdʑoː<t:登場> ɕita<t:した。> ↘/}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Katarites.} The \emph{Kokoro Katarite} maps YomiScript IPA to Kokoro's phoneme inventory and runs the
model's three ONNX parts (text encoder with duration prediction, F0/energy prediction, decoder) in the browser or in
Node. The \emph{Supertonic Katarite} gives the engine text: each span as written, except spans whose written form (or
a 2--8 character form inside it) has more than one reading in a list of 12{,}875 forms derived from
IPAdic~\citep{ipadic}, or that contain numbers, Latin letters, symbols or kanji numerals; those are given as kana from
the YomiScript reading (は as わ, long vowels as ー). The list and the rule belong to the Katarite; the Yomite writes
only YomiScript. The Katarite chooses no reading: the kana come from the YomiScript, and the spans given as written are
read by the engine's own acoustic model, which is where this Katarite still depends on the engine's reading.

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Sets.} (1)~\emph{ja}: 24 Japanese texts written to probe Kokoro's front end (punctuation, questions, numbers,
loanwords, Latin script, a long passage); (2)~\emph{en}: 22 English texts of the same kind; (3)~\emph{hard}: 70
Japanese sentences, 35 homograph sentences, 12 with acronyms, 12 with numbers and 11 with dates, with gold readings in
katakana. The gold readings were reviewed by one native speaker of the project, not by independent annotators
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Yomites.} Two LLMs through their command-line agents with all tools disabled: Claude Code
(\texttt{claude-opus-5-5}) and Codex (\texttt{gpt-6.1-sol}), as reported by the CLIs. One call per set, with a prompt
giving the subset of YomiScript Kokoro needs (IPA, word spaces, \ys{‖}, \ys{↘}/\ys{↗}, no pitch values or accent marks,
since Kokoro predicts prosody), the Japanese mora-to-IPA table of the specification, the text layer, and five examples
written by Kokoro's own front end on sentences outside the test sets. Baselines: Kokoro's original front end (misaki,
via KPipeline) and pyopenjtalk~0.4.1 (the same OpenJTalk and NAIST-JDIC front end that piper-plus compiles to
WebAssembly; we use it as piper-plus's stand-in for readings).

\paragraph{Prompt rounds.} The prompt was revised twice, for notation only: the first round gave too little of it (the
LLMs wrote phonemic \ys{ni} for に where the specification writes \ys{ɲi}, and several utterances per item); the second
added the specification's mora table and the one-utterance rule; the third added the text layer. The instruction about
readings (``each word read the way it is meant in its context'') did not change. All numbers are from the third round
unless stated otherwise. Since the revisions were made after seeing results on the same sets, the hard-set numbers are
development results, not results on untouched test data.

\paragraph{Scoring.} Every output goes through the Kokoro Katarite's planner to the phoneme string Kokoro is given. On
\emph{ja} and \emph{en} we compare with the original front end; on \emph{hard} with the gold. A reading counts as
\emph{the same} when the phonemes match after removing word spaces and punctuation and merging spellings that do not
change the reading: long-vowel spelling (イイ/イー), Kokoro's ɨ for a devoiced ɯ, the allophone written for a moraic
nasal, and h/n before i written without their palatal allophone (for English: stress marks, Kokoro's reduced vowels and
flap). These merges were chosen while looking at the outputs; they are to be frozen before an untouched test. Intelligibility is measured by transcribing the audio with Whisper large-v3-turbo~\citep{radford2023whisper},
converting the transcript to katakana with pyopenjtalk, and computing the kana error rate against what the system was
meant to say (English: word error rate against the text).

\paragraph{Commercial TTS.} The newest TTS model of each of three services read the plain text of the hard set, three
times each, since they do not always read a sentence the same way: OpenAI \texttt{gpt-4o-mini-tts} (snapshot
2025-12-15, voice coral), Google \texttt{gemini-3.8-flash-tts} (voice Kore) and ElevenLabs \texttt{eleven\_v3}, with
ElevenLabs' previous \texttt{eleven\_multilingual\_v2} for comparison (voice Rachel), all on 31 October 2026. Their
readings can only be judged from the audio, and so are ours here: the LLMs' YomiScript went through the tools' pipeline
(accent marks from a dictionary, \texttt{@yomiscript/ja-accent}: OpenJTalk's analysis of the original text marks only
the accent phrases whose reading agrees with the YomiScript, so the reading is never changed; then Kokoro), and
Kokoro's original pipeline read the plain text, one run each.

\paragraph{Judging a reading from audio.} Whisper is not a usable judge of readings: it writes most of what it hears in
kanji and digits, even when prompted in kana, and its language model leans to the expected words, so a misread 一日
is transcribed as 一日 again. We use a speech recogniser that outputs hiragana and has no language model (wav2vec~2.0
XLSR-53 fine-tuned on Japanese, \texttt{vumichien/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-japanese-hiragana}) on minimal pairs. For each
of 69 target words in 62 of the 70 sentences, we list the right readings and the plausible misreadings (一日:
\emph{tsuitachi} vs \emph{ichinichi}); each candidate is the gold reading of the sentence with only that word changed,
and the candidate with the highest CTC likelihood is the reading heard. A sentence is read right when all its target
words are. The other eight sentences are not judged, since every plausible reading of them counts as right (今日 in
今日の日本経済, 紅葉, SIP, NHK, and plain numbers). We checked the judge on the recordings whose spoken phonemes we
know (the original Kokoro, and the LLMs' YomiScript through Kokoro): it agrees with the known reading on 193 of 207
words (93.2\%). Of the 14 disagreements, 13 are right readings judged as misreadings and one is a misreading judged
right (the original Kokoro's 8分 read \emph{hachi-pun}); on these recordings the judge mostly understated accuracy. The check covers Kokoro's audio only: on commercial speech the judge's
error was not measured and may go either way. Two other judges were rejected on the same check: Whisper's
transcripts, and a multilingual forced aligner over romanised letters (MMS), which agreed on 71\% of whole sentences and 90\% of minimal pairs and favoured the
shorter candidate (a per-letter bonus tuned on the same recordings raised it, but could not be checked independently).

\paragraph{Context.} To measure how much context the Yomite needs, we wrote 50 chat scenarios: 25 homographs (上手,
人気, 生花, 風車, 最中, 心中 and others; not 一日, which the instructions use as an example), each with two user messages
whose context fixes one reading, ending with ``answer in one line, using the word X''. Three LLMs through their CLIs
with no tools, Claude Code (\texttt{claude-opus-5-5}), Codex (\texttt{gpt-6.1-sol}) and Antigravity
(\texttt{gemini-4-argon-medium}), all given the YomiScript instructions our tools give, played three roles on the same
answers: the \emph{author} answered the user and wrote its answer as YomiScript in the same call, as with a speak
tool; a \emph{reader} (each of the three LLMs, in a new call) wrote YomiScript for the answer alone, or for the answer
with the user's message. The word's reading was taken from the YomiScript through the text layer and compared with
the scenario's reading; pyopenjtalk read the same answers. One run: 150 author calls and 900 reader calls.
A second, larger experiment addressed this one's weaknesses (few words, answers that restated the context, no text
from elsewhere). For 65 homographs (21 of the earlier words and 44 new ones) we wrote 130 items: both items of a word
share one short target sentence that is ambiguous alone (「それは生花です。」), and each has a preceding paragraph,
like a web page or a document, and a user's message that fix one reading without containing the word or its reading;
a script checked the latter. Without the context a reader can thus get at most one of the two right. The three LLMs
wrote YomiScript for the target sentence as external text, with and without the paragraph, and as a fixed chat answer,
with and without the user's message; as authors they answered each user's message in at most 15 characters, and every
LLM read every author's answer with and without the message. Ten items per call, no two of a word in one call. The
primary comparison (external text, with minus without the paragraph, pooled over the LLMs) was stated before the
run.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The Kokoro Katarite reproduces the original}\label{sec:parity}

Given YomiScript written from Kokoro's own front end, the Katarite hands Kokoro the same input as the original
pipeline (Table~\ref{tab:parity}). With fp32 weights the audio's correlation with the original is similar to that of
a second run of the original (its decoder draws random numbers): slightly higher in the English mean and the Japanese
minimum, slightly lower in the Japanese mean and the English minimum. The fp16-weight model used in the browser is
close on average, but its lowest correlations (0.85 and 0.80) are well below those of a second run; we have not
listened for the difference. Exact reproduction needs two things beyond the notation's core: the planner's \emph{original}
mode, which renders a pause and an inner sentence end as the original front end does (by default the Katarite gives
Kokoro a comma and a period there, where its Japanese voices pause), and \ys{<p:…>}, which passes through punctuation
the model uses as a prosodic cue. This makes the comparisons below about readings, not about a different synthesizer.

To see what the container adds, the three LLMs wrote the hard set as YomiScript, as kana (the setting of
\citet{koriyama2026g2p}), as SSML with an IPA \texttt{phoneme} element on every word, and as the text with kana added

prompts and the same five examples (Table~\ref{tab:representation}). For Japanese alone the container does not decide
the reading: kana was read right in every sentence of every run that returned an answer, at about a fifth of
YomiScript's characters and less than half its time, and sparse kana was close behind at a quarter of the characters.
YomiScript's and SSML's misses were mostly slips in writing IPA (Latin \ys{u} for \ys{ɯ}, a lost \ys{ʲ} or long
vowel), not other readings. SSML's one element per word split geminates across elements (\ys{moɾat} + た), which is
not well-formed Japanese IPA; it cost four times YomiScript's output, was the slowest, and had the most malformed
items among the answers that came back (kana's 7.8 per call is the one call that returned nothing). With one element per
bunsetsu instead (\texttt{eval/representation\_isolated}; Claude and Codex, three runs), no element ended in a split
geminate, accuracy was 96.2\% and the size 197 characters, 2.6 times YomiScript's. Since all 70 sentences went in one
call, the two sentences of a homograph could inform each other; rerun in calls of 10 with no shared word, the scores
moved by under a point (YomiScript 97.4\%, kana 99.3\%; paired differences $-$0.5 [$-$2.9, +1.7] and $-$0.7
[$-$1.9, 0.0]), and the only reading that changed was h001 市, read \emph{shi} by Claude's kana in two runs.
Sparse kana is only as good as the front end on what it leaves unannotated (pyopenjtalk read 日本 as \emph{nippon}
in every run), and it needs that front end in the synthesizer. The scoring is not equally strict: kana goes through
the converter the gold itself was written with, while YomiScript and SSML are judged on the LLM's own IPA.

What YomiScript adds is therefore not accuracy on Japanese readings but what kana cannot carry, and we measured two
such things with the Kokoro Katarite (\texttt{eval/ipa\_value}; one voice, nobody has listened yet). \emph{Accent}: on
44 words in 21 Tokyo minimal sets (雨/飴, 橋/箸/端, 酒/鮭, \dots) in sentences that fix the word, the three LLMs, asked
for accent marks, placed the accent as the dictionary (OpenJTalk) does in 96--98\% of words over three runs; the one
word they disagreed on, 潮, is one where dictionaries disagree. With the marks, the difference between the two members
of a pair went the expected way in Kokoro's pitch at 92--95\% of the measured points (median 4.5--5 semitones), against
42\% with Kokoro's own pitch and 19\% for kana, whose accent a dictionary can only guess from the katakana (it found
the gold accent of 15 of the 44 words). The same holds when the marks come from the dictionary on the original text,
which the text layer allows; whole-sentence kana has lost that text. \emph{Other languages}: in 16 Japanese sentences
with English meant as English (quoted phrases, titles, names), the LLMs put every English target in an \ys{en-US}
section and none of 8 loanword controls; Whisper recognised 96--99\% of the English words from those sections against
65--75\% from the same stretch of the kana version (a capability check, not a listening test). YomiScript is also
already the phonemes a synthesizer such as Kokoro takes, and keeps the original Kokoro's audio.

\subsection{Text layer and cost}

Joining the \ys{<t:…>} values gave back the input exactly (whitespace ignored) in all 232 outputs (2 LLMs $\times$ 116

\subsection{Context: other Yomites we tried}

In an earlier experiment (7 Japanese items) the LLMs wrote complete YomiScript with accent marks and pitch values from
plain text; their accent-nucleus F1 against a rule-based reference was 0.82 (Claude) and 0.60 (Codex), on the 4 and 3
items whose phonemes lined up with the reference, and adding pitch
values to YomiScript whose marks were given was easy for both. Accent from plain text is the weaker part, so here the
Katarite's engine predicts prosody. A dedicated 7.7M-parameter reading model (Yomi-0) trained on
300k sentences labelled by pyopenjtalk stayed below its teacher (JSUT~\citep{sonobe2017jsut,jsutlabel} sentence
accuracy 68.2\% vs 72.5\%; hard set 57.1\% vs 71.4\%, both under the earlier strict, spelling-sensitive scoring,
under which pyopenjtalk's 78.6\% of Table~\ref{tab:hard} is 71.4\%). A student distilled from a dictionary front end
is unlikely to read much better than its teacher's local-context readings, but we have not separated this from the student's capacity,
data, training or our scoring as the cause of the gap.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Why it works.} A dictionary front end chooses from the local context of the sentence; an LLM reads the
meaning, and can use as much context as it is given. On self-contained sentences (Table~\ref{tab:hard}) the sentence
was enough. Where it is not, the surrounding text decides (Section~\ref{sec:author}): on sentences ambiguous alone
the LLMs went from 48.5\% to 97.7\% with the preceding paragraph, and a reader given the user's message read a terse
answer as well as its author (99.0\% each), with no difference detected. So the Yomite need not be the writer; it needs the context. Text from elsewhere (a web page, a document, a
message) can be read as well as the context passed with it allows. When the LLM writes the text itself, it has the
context in the same call, while a separate Yomite has it only if the conversation is passed along, at the cost of
another call over it.

\paragraph{What it buys for the browser.} No dictionary front end ships: the browser needs only the Katarite
(Kokoro with fp16 weights, 163\,MB plus a 0.5\,MB voice; Supertonic~3, 385\,MB). The dictionary accent of the tools
(\texttt{@yomiscript/ja-accent}, OpenJTalk with a 28\,MB dictionary) is optional and decides no reading; it was not
used for Tables~\ref{tab:hard}, \ref{tab:representation} and~\ref{tab:asr}, and for the commercial comparison and the
browser measurements below it was run beforehand in Node. Compared with shipping OpenJTalk
(piper-plus: about 19\,MB of WebAssembly, by its documentation), the gain is not size but readings. We measured the
Kokoro Katarite in headless Chromium~130 on one laptop (Apple M2 Max; 3 runs, medians; \texttt{eval/browser\_bench}).
A cold load transfers 186\,MB (with the 22\,MB ONNX Runtime WebAssembly), about 15\,s at 100\,Mbit/s by size; served
locally, the engine was ready in 1.7\,s and the first 2\,s sentence was synthesized 2.8\,s after opening the page.
The real-time factor was 1.60 on one WebAssembly thread, 0.54 on four, and 0.06 with the decoder on WebGPU; with every
part on WebGPU the durations came out wrong, so we keep the encoder on WebAssembly. The tab's renderer process
used about 1.7--1.9\,GB, set by loading the models rather than by the length of the speech. The browser's HTTP cache did
not keep the 107\,MB decoder between loads; storing the files with the Cache Storage API did. These are one machine
and one browser, not a phone.

\paragraph{Delivery.} The same idea fits a Model Context Protocol~\citep{mcp} server or a chat-client plugin: the
calling LLM writes YomiScript as the argument of a \texttt{speak} tool, and the client can show the text from the text
layer while it is spoken. The TTS has no LLM, and with the Kokoro Katarite no front end of its own.

\paragraph{Costs.} Writing the reading needs no separate model call, but it is not free: the calling LLM writes
5.6--6.1 times the characters of the Japanese text (2.7--3.2 without the text layer), which costs output tokens and
delays the first audio. We measured the synthesizer's side (above) but not the time the LLM takes to write the
YomiScript before the first sentence can be spoken; streaming per sentence and a lighter
notation (for example omitting the text layer when not displayed) are ways to reduce it. For Japanese-only text to a
kana-input synthesizer, kana is the cheaper container (Section~\ref{sec:representation}); a Yomite could write kana
where that suffices and leave the conversion to IPA to a deterministic step.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The following weaken the claims above, and Section~\ref{sec:results} should be read with them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gold from the project (weakens Table~\ref{tab:hard}).} The hard set and its gold readings were written by
  the project and reviewed by one native speaker of the project, not by independent annotators; LLM assistance in writing them cannot be
  ruled out, which could favour the LLMs. Several items admit two readings; one scored with one only is h001
  シアトルの市では (\emph{ichi}, the market; \emph{shi}, the city, is also possible), which counts against the
  commercial systems that read \emph{shi}.
  \item \textbf{Small sets (weakens all tables).} 35 homograph sentences and 70 hard sentences; one wrong answer moves
  the homograph score by 2.9 points. Table~\ref{tab:hard} has no confidence interval; those of the
  other tables are wide.
  \item \textbf{The judge of Table~\ref{tab:commercial}.} It was checked on Kokoro's audio only, where it erred against
  the right reading on 14 of 207 words; its errors on natural commercial speech are not measured, and the misreadings it
  found have not yet been confirmed by listening. The target words and their misreadings were listed by the project,
  partly from the misreadings the systems produced.
  \item \textbf{Commercial services.} One voice per service and one date; the readings may differ by voice, and the
  services change their models without notice (we pinned OpenAI's snapshot; the others have no snapshot).
  \item \textbf{One run per LLM (weakens Tables~\ref{tab:hard}--\ref{tab:asr}).} LLM outputs vary between calls:
  run three times, the same prompt gave 94.3--100\% of the hard set per run (Section~\ref{sec:representation}).
  \item \textbf{Prompt revisions (Table~\ref{tab:hard} is a development result).} The prompt was revised twice after
  seeing results, for notation; the reading instruction did not change, but the revision was informed by the same sets,
  and no untouched test set was scored with the final prompt.
  \item \textbf{Frontier LLMs only.} Two on the hard set and three in Section~\ref{sec:author}; smaller or local models,
  which an MCP server will also meet, were not tested.
  \item \textbf{The context experiments (weaken Tables~\ref{tab:author} and~\ref{tab:context}).} One run each; the
  scenarios and items were written by the project (possibly with LLM help) with right readings reviewed by one native speaker of the project, and the
  larger experiment's author comparison covers 210 of 390 answers. In the first, the scenarios ask for the word and a short answer, which makes the case where the
  context matters more common than in free conversation.
  \item \textbf{Intelligibility is a proxy (weakens Table~\ref{tab:asr}).} ASR transcripts, read back with pyopenjtalk,
  carry both systems' errors; naturalness was not measured.
  \item \textbf{Baselines.} pyopenjtalk stands in for piper-plus; context-aware Japanese G2P models were not compared.
  \item \textbf{Browser measurements.} One laptop and headless Chromium~130, models served locally; no phone, Safari
  or Firefox, and nobody has listened to the WebGPU audio (it was checked against WebAssembly by length and envelope).
  \item \textbf{Long utterances.} The Kokoro Katarite does not yet split an utterance longer than Kokoro's 510 phonemes;
  the one long passage could not be spoken from a single LLM utterance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

An LLM given the context can decide how text is read, and a notation built on IPA and BCP~47 can carry that decision
to a small synthesizer with no front end of its own. On our development set this let a browser TTS speak Japanese
homographs that pyopenjtalk misread and the numbers, dates and acronyms that Kokoro's own front end misread, and on an
independent benchmark the LLMs' readings had fewer errors than pyopenjtalk's. In our experiments the context
explained the gain, and no effect of authorship was detected once a reader had it: given the preceding paragraph or the user's message, LLMs read sentences that are
ambiguous alone almost always right (97.7--98.2\%, against 48.5\% without), with no difference detected from the author of a terse
answer, who has the conversation without a second call. By an automatic judge not yet checked by listening, the
newest commercial TTS models, which read the text themselves, were right on 72.6--85.5\% of the judged hard sentences.
These results are preliminary and small; the next step is an independent, larger evaluation with frozen prompts and
human listening tests.
